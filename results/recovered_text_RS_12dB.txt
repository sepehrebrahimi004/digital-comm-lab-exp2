Every wireless link is a negotiation between three resources: power, bandwidth and reliability. Claude Shannon showed in 1948 that for any channel there is a maximum rate, the capacity, below which information can be sent with an arbitrarily small probability of error. His proof did not say how to build such a system, and engineers spent the next fifty years searching for codes that come close to the limit. Convolutional codes and Reed-Solomon codes made deep-space missions and compact discs possible. Turbo codes in the nineties and low-density parity-check codes, rediscovered at the same time, finally brought practical systems within a fraction of a decibel of capacity.
A modern receiver therefore does far more than slice symbols. It estimates the channel, tracks the carrier phase, computes soft reliability values for every bit and lets an iterative decoder exchange those values until the code constraints are satisfied. Reed-Solomon codes still matter because they correct whole symbols: a burst of consecutive bit errors that falls inside a few bytes costs only a few symbol corrections.
